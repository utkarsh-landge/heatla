01 — Provenance

Real data, every column sourced.

The target is UCLA Heat Maps’ estimate of excess heat-related ER visits per 10,000 residents, from its CalHeatScore level 4 layer: a total over May–October 2008–2018 that covers every heat day up to and including level 4. It is a model-based estimate published by UCLA, pulled from public ArcGIS layers. Nothing on this page is synthetic.

06 — 2030 scenario

A 2030 scaling scenario.

Each ZIP’s UCLA label multiplied by its Cal-Adapt heat factor — the ratio of projected extreme-heat days (2026–2035) to the 2008–2018 baseline, from a 32-model climate ensemble. This is a what-if calculation, not a prediction from the models above and not part of their evaluation.

Formula. scenario rate = UCLA label × (mean extreme-heat days 2026–2035 ÷ mean extreme-heat days 2008–2018). Assumptions. ER visits per heat day, population and community characteristics stay as they were in 2008–2018; only the number of extreme-heat days changes. Cal-Adapt counts days above the local 98th-percentile temperature, which is a different heat definition from the CalHeatScore levels behind the label. Source: Cal-Adapt exheat_year_ens32avg (LOCA, 32 models).

07 — Cooling centers

Where to open them.

Pick a budget and radius. Benefit = out-of-fold predicted excess ER visits within range of an open center. The weights are Part 2 out-of-fold Poisson predictions (augmented LA model). Exact solves an integer program; greedy adds the best next site. Scored on the UCLA label, these plans cover on average 0.25 points less burden than plans chosen with the label itself, and 0.8 points more than plans weighted by population alone.

08 — Honesty

Limitations.

    A prototype research study, not an operational tool. See REPORT.md for the honest write-up.

    Heat is not felt equally.

    The same storm of extreme heat lands hardest on the ZIP codes with the least cooling, the oldest residents, and the fewest cars. This is a first attempt to say where — and to act on it.
